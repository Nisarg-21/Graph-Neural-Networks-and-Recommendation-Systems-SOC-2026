# Q3. Why Do We Need Self-Loops?

## Part 1 — Eigenvalues of $D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}$ lie in $[-1, 1]$

We're given that the normalized Laplacian $L = I_N - D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}$ has eigenvalues in $[0, 2]$.

Let $S = D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}$. Then $L = I_N - S$, which rearranges to $S = I_N - L$.

$S$ and $L$ share the same eigenvectors. If $v$ is an eigenvector of $L$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, then

$$S v = (I_N - L)v = v - \lambda v = (1 - \lambda)\,v,$$

so $v$ is also an eigenvector of $S$ with eigenvalue $1 - \lambda$. Every eigenvalue of $S$ is therefore $1 - \lambda$ for some eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $L$.

Since $\lambda \in [0, 2]$:

$$1 - \lambda \in [\,1 - 2,\; 1 - 0\,] = [-1, 1].$$

So all eigenvalues of $D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}$ lie in $[-1, 1]$. $\blacksquare$

## Part 3 — What a node loses without the self-loop

Without $I_N$ added to $A$, the aggregation at a node sums only over its neighbors, the node's own current features never appear in the sum. So at every layer the node throws away what it knew about itself and rebuilds its embedding purely from the neighborhood.

After a few layers the node's own signal is gone entirely, and its representation reflects only the surrounding structure, not its own attributes. Adding the self-loop $I_N$ keeps the node inside its own update, so its features get *mixed with* the neighbors' instead of *replaced by* them.
